Creating a Community Food Forest with Cultivated Commons

A Landowner’s Field Guide

This guide is an invitation.

Cultivated Commons is a land trust rooted in the belief that the land beneath our feet is a commons — belonging, in the deepest sense, to the whole web of life that depends on it. We work with landowners across Henderson County to cultivate food forests, riparian corridors, and living demonstration sites: places where the ecological and the communal reinforce each other.

We are not here to take your land or lock it away. We are here because land — your land — might become something remarkable with the right partnership, and we want you to know every option available to you.

This guide walks through the full range of ways a landowner can work with Cultivated Commons, from the lightest handshake agreement to full donation of the property. Every path is voluntary. Every path carries different benefits, responsibilities, and levels of protection. We want you to find the one that fits your life and your vision for your land.

Wherever you are in this conversation — just curious, actively considering, or ready to move — there is a page here for you.

Front matter

What we cultivate

Cultivated Commons is building a living network of food forests, riparian corridors, and community-tended land across Western North Carolina. Our work is grounded in five beliefs:

Ecological Abundance

Healthy land is productive land. We cultivate diverse, multi-layered food forests and native plant communities that feed people, support pollinators, filter water, and build soil — all at once.

Indigenous Land Stewardship

We draw on the cultivation traditions of the Cherokee and other Indigenous peoples of this region: the understanding that human care and ecological health are not opposites, but partners. We don't aim to restore land to some imagined pristine state — we aim to cultivate it, actively and responsibly, for the long haul.

Community Access

The food forest is not a private garden. It is a commons — a place neighbors can walk through, harvest from, learn in, and tend together. We work to ensure that the land we steward is genuinely accessible to the surrounding community.

Watershed & Water Health

We site our work with special attention to creeks, springs, and floodplains. Riparian corridors are the connective tissue of our landscape. Every site we steward is an opportunity to slow water, filter runoff, and knit the watershed back together.

Permanent Protection

We are in this for generations, not grant cycles. Whatever agreement we make with a landowner, we take our stewardship responsibility seriously — showing up, documenting, tending, and defending the work we've built together.

Front matter

How it works

Every CC partnership starts with a conversation and moves at the landowner's pace. Here is what the typical arc looks like:

  1. First Conversation

    We listen more than we talk. We want to understand what you love about your land, what you worry about, and what you hope for. No forms, no obligations.

  2. Site Visit

    We walk the land with you. Our land steward documents ecological features — soils, hydrology, plant communities, access points — and begins imagining what cultivation might look like here. We share our observations and ask yours.

  3. Fit Assessment

    We sit with what we've learned and ask honestly whether this site is a strong candidate for CC stewardship. If it isn't, we say so plainly. If it is, we move forward.

  4. Partnership Design

    We identify the legal vehicle that fits your situation and your goals — from a simple MOU to a full conservation easement or property transfer. We draft the terms together.

  5. Documentation & Closing

    We complete the baseline documentation report, any surveys or title work required, and the formal agreement. You receive a copy of everything.

  6. Ongoing Stewardship

    We show up — annually at minimum, more often as the site warrants. We document, tend, and report back to you. The land stays healthy. The relationship stays alive.

Front matter

Is this site a good fit?

We don't chase any parcel that comes our way. Good stewardship requires honest discernment — for your sake and ours. These are the qualities we look for:

Ecological Integrity

Does the land have living soil, native plant presence, or hydrological function worth building on? It doesn't have to be pristine — but it needs a foundation.

Food Forest Potential

Is there enough sun, space, and soil to establish a meaningful food-producing system? Even a quarter-acre can become a productive commons with the right design.

Water & Watershed Connection

Does the site touch a creek, spring, wetland, or significant drainage? Proximity to water multiplies the ecological value of everything we plant.

Community Reach

Can neighbors, students, or the broader public realistically get to and use this land? A site that's hard to reach or invisible to the community is harder to tend and harder to love.

Ecological Corridor Value

Does the site connect to other natural areas, greenways, or riparian corridors? Land that bridges fragments is worth more to the ecosystem than an island.

Landowner Partnership

Do we sense a genuine shared vision? We need a landowner who is invested in the outcome, not just willing to sign something.

Tenure Potential

Is there a realistic path to a durable agreement — at least long enough to establish and harvest a food forest? Temporary arrangements on unstable ground are hard for both parties.

Mission Alignment

Does this site advance our core work — food access, ecological health, Indigenous cultivation practices, public education? Not every good piece of land is the right land for CC.

Stewardship Capacity

Can we actually tend this site well given our current capacity? Taking on more than we can steward honors no one.

Fee Simple Donation

The most complete form of land protection — a full gift of the property to Cultivated Commons.

What it means

You transfer full ownership of the land to CC. The land becomes part of the CC commons permanently, stewarded for food, ecology, and community access in perpetuity. You no longer carry the responsibilities or liabilities of ownership.

What you keep

The satisfaction of knowing exactly what becomes of your land. Depending on the terms, you or your family may retain certain use rights — a garden plot, a path, access to the orchard. We negotiate those together.

Why landowners choose this

  • The strongest possible guarantee that the land will be cared for and protected
  • Significant income and estate tax benefits (see Tax Benefits)
  • Freedom from ongoing property taxes, maintenance liability, and ownership burden
  • A named legacy — your family's contribution to the commons, remembered

Fee Simple Bargain Sale

A below-market sale of the property to CC — part gift, part transaction.

Protection

What it means

You sell the land to CC at a price below its appraised fair market value. The difference between what you receive and what the land is worth becomes a charitable contribution — and a potential tax deduction. CC gains full ownership, and the land is permanently protected.

What you keep

A cash payment for your land. The amount is negotiated, and it can be as low as covering your cost basis or closing costs, or higher. The gap between sale price and appraised value is the "donated" portion.

Why landowners choose this

  • You need some return from the land but also want it protected
  • Partial tax deduction on the donated portion (see Tax Benefits)
  • Cleaner than a full gift if you have equity tied up in the property
  • Same permanent protection as a full donation

Conservation Easement

You keep ownership. A permanent legal agreement protects the land's ecological values forever.

Protection
High
Landowner keeps title
Yes
Duration
Permanent

What it means

A conservation easement is a voluntary legal agreement between you and CC that permanently limits certain uses of your land — to protect its ecological, agricultural, or community values. The easement is recorded on the deed and runs with the land, binding all future owners. You stay in title. CC holds and enforces the easement.

What you keep

Full ownership, the right to live on and use the land, the ability to sell or pass it on to heirs. What changes is that certain uses — subdivision, large-scale development, industrial activity — are permanently off the table.

Why landowners choose this

  • You want to protect the land but aren't ready to give it up
  • Potential income tax deduction on the easement's appraised value (see Tax Benefits)
  • Significant estate tax benefits; your heirs may inherit at reduced value
  • The land stays in your family and community for generations
  • A relationship with CC for ongoing ecological stewardship

Legal vehicle 3 of 7

Life Estate / Remainder Interest

You live on the land for the rest of your life. CC receives the property when you pass.

Protection
High

Yes (for life)

What it means

You deed the property to CC now, but retain a "life estate" — the legal right to live on and use the land for the rest of your life (and, if you choose, the lives of named family members). At your death, CC receives full ownership automatically, without going through probate.

What you keep

Everything during your lifetime — your home, your garden, your routines. The transfer to CC happens after, seamlessly.

Why landowners choose this

  • You want CC to have the land, but you're not ready to leave it
  • Avoids probate and simplifies estate planning
  • You may claim a charitable deduction now, based on the present value of CC's remainder interest
  • Your heirs don't inherit a property management burden
  • Peace of mind: the land's future is decided

Legal vehicle 5 of 7

Long-Term Recorded Lease

CC stewards your land for a generation or more — without taking ownership.

Protection
Moderate
Landowner keeps title
Yes
Duration
25–99 years

What it means

You lease your land to CC for a term of 25 to 99 years, recorded on the deed at the county register of deeds. CC gains the right to cultivate, improve, and manage the land during the lease term. You retain full ownership and reclaim the land — and everything CC has built on it — when the lease ends.

What you keep

Title to the land, and the right to pass it on to your heirs. The lease does not transfer ownership — only stewardship rights for the term.

Why landowners choose this

  • You want CC actively tending the land but aren't ready (or willing) to give it up
  • A multi-decade lease gives CC the stability needed to plant and cultivate a mature food forest
  • No permanent loss of ownership
  • A recorded lease protects CC's investment if the land is sold or inherited during the term
  • Negotiable terms: you and CC shape what CC can and cannot do

Legal vehicle 6 of 7

Deed Restriction / Covenant

A permanent land-use promise recorded directly on your deed.

Protection
Moderate
Landowner keeps title
Yes
Duration
Permanent

What it means

A deed restriction (or restrictive covenant) is a legal limitation on land use that you record on the deed and that binds all future owners. Unlike a conservation easement, it does not require a formal holder like CC to enforce it — though CC can be named as an interested party with enforcement rights. Common restrictions: no subdivision beyond a certain number of parcels, no commercial development, no removal of the riparian buffer.

What you keep

Full ownership and full use within the restrictions. You can sell, give, or bequeath the land — the restriction follows.

Why landowners choose this

  • You want a specific protection — not a full conservation easement — attached to the land permanently
  • Simpler and less costly than a full easement
  • Useful alongside other agreements (a deed restriction can complement an MOU or lease)
  • No annual monitoring requirement from CC

MOU / License Agreement

The lightest touch — a flexible, revocable collaboration that either party can exit.

What it means

A Memorandum of Understanding or License Agreement is an informal written agreement that lets CC work on your land — planting, tending, bringing volunteers, running programming — without any transfer of rights or restrictions on the deed. It lives only on paper, between you and CC, and can be updated or ended by either party with reasonable notice.

What you keep

Everything. No deed changes, no restrictions, no permanence unless you both want it. You can end the agreement if circumstances change.

Why landowners choose this

  • You want to try out a partnership before making any long-term commitment
  • You have full confidence in CC but aren't ready for a formal legal vehicle
  • The land situation is in flux (pending sale, estate, family decision) and you can't commit to anything permanent
  • You want CC's knowledge and hands on your land, with minimal paperwork

Costs & Funding

Every CC partnership involves some costs. We are transparent about them. The table below shows what each legal vehicle typically requires, organized by category: what is essential for every project, what is ideal, and what is optional depending on your goals.

Essential — required for every project regardless of vehicle

  • Site visit (CC covers this)
  • Baseline Documentation Report (landowner cost; CC covers for MOUs and leases)
  • Title search (required for any deed change; typically $300–$800)
  • Recording fees at the Register of Deeds ($26–$100 depending on document length)

Ideal — strongly recommended to support the work and protect both parties

  • Survey and legal description ($800–$3,000 depending on acreage and terrain; required if easement or deed change covers only a portion of the property)
  • Independent legal counsel for the landowner ($500–$2,000; we strongly recommend this for all deed-changing vehicles)
  • Stewardship & Defense Contribution (required for conservation easements; see Stewardship & Defense, p. XX)

Optional — only needed if you want specific benefits

  • Appraisal ($2,500–$6,000): required to claim a charitable income tax deduction for an easement donation, bargain sale, fee simple donation, or life estate. Paid by the landowner.
  • IRS Form 8283 preparation: your tax advisor handles this; nominal cost.

What CC's Commons Fund can cover

For qualifying projects, CC's Commons Fund can help with title search, survey, and closing costs. Eligibility is based on the project's ecological significance, community benefit, and CC's available capital. Ask us early — we would rather help you plan than scramble at closing.

Tax Benefits

Land protection can generate real financial benefits for landowners — but the rules are specific and the amounts vary. This page gives a plain-language overview. Always consult your own tax advisor before making decisions based on potential tax benefits.

Income Tax Deduction (Federal)

If you donate land or an interest in land to a qualifying nonprofit like CC, you may deduct the appraised value of that donation from your federal income tax. The donation must be documented with a qualified appraisal.

VehicleDeductible?Deductible Amount
Fee Simple DonationYesFull appraised value of the property
Fee Simple Bargain SaleYesDifference between appraised value and sale price
Life EstateYesPresent value of CC's remainder interest (calculated)
Conservation EasementYesAppraised reduction in property value
Long-Term LeaseNo—
Deed RestrictionPossiblyConsult your attorney
MOU / LicenseNo—

Deduction limits

The federal deduction for conservation easements and land donations is generally limited to 50% of your adjusted gross income (AGI) per year, with a 15-year carryforward. Qualified farmers and ranchers may deduct up to 100% of AGI. NC provides an additional state income tax credit (currently 25% of the donated value, up to $500,000) for qualifying easement donations.

Estate Tax Benefits

Property subject to a conservation easement or donated to CC may be valued lower in your estate, reducing estate tax exposure. For large holdings, this can be significant. An estate planning attorney can model the impact for your specific situation.

Property Tax

Conservation easements in NC may qualify for the Present Use Value program, reducing property tax assessment. Land donated to CC (a nonprofit) is removed from the tax rolls entirely.

NC Tax Credit for Conservation Easements

North Carolina offers a 25% income tax credit (up to $500,000) for qualifying conservation easement donations — separate from and stackable with the federal deduction. This is among the strongest state-level incentives in the country.

Stewardship & Defense Contribution

For conservation easements, CC asks landowners to make a one-time contribution to our Stewardship & Defense Fund — a permanent endowment that ensures CC can monitor and, when necessary, defend your easement long into the future.

Why it matters

A conservation easement is only as strong as the organization holding it. If CC cannot fund annual monitoring visits, or if a future owner violates the easement and CC cannot afford to take legal action, the protection you created is meaningless. The Stewardship & Defense Fund ensures neither of those things happens.

How it's calculated

CC uses the Land Trust Alliance's standard calculator, which accounts for the property's size, complexity, and estimated long-term monitoring and legal costs. The result is a one-time contribution, not an annual fee. Typical contributions range from $5,000 to $30,000 depending on acreage and site complexity.

Is it tax-deductible?

Yes — contributions to the Stewardship & Defense Fund are tax-deductible charitable donations.

Can CC help with the contribution?

In some cases, CC's Commons Fund can provide a partial match or grant for qualifying easements. We discuss this during the partnership design phase — before closing, not at the table.

What does CC do with it?

The principal is invested in a restricted fund. Only the interest is used for monitoring and defense. The contribution is permanent — it stays with the easement even if CC ever merges with or transfers the easement to another accredited land trust.

The Stewardship & Defense Contribution is required only for conservation easements. It is not required for leases, MOUs, deed restrictions, or property transfers.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to give up my land to work with Cultivated Commons?

No. Most of our partnerships begin with something light — a conversation, a site visit, a simple MOU. A full property transfer is one option at one end of a spectrum. You choose what level of involvement makes sense for you.

What happens to the food that's grown?

It's for the community. CC designs food forests for open-harvest access — neighbors, students, anyone who shows up. We do not sell the harvest. We do not require advance sign-up. We want food to be available to whoever is hungry and nearby.

Will CC build things on my land? Do I get a say?

Yes, and yes. Any CC work on your land is negotiated with you: what gets planted, where, by whom, on what timeline. We are guests on your land until the terms of any agreement say otherwise.

What if I change my mind?

That depends on what you've signed. An MOU can be ended by either party. A conservation easement or deed transfer is permanent. This is one reason we suggest starting lighter and building trust before committing to anything irrevocable.

How does CC make money? Are you going to sell my land?

CC is a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. We do not sell land we hold — that would violate our mission and our accreditation obligations. We are funded through donations, grants, and the stewardship contributions landowners make as part of formal agreements. Our financial statements are public.

I have a mortgage. Can I still do a conservation easement?

Often yes, but the lender must sign a mortgage subordination agreement — a document saying the lender agrees the easement takes precedence over their mortgage in the event of foreclosure. Most lenders cooperate; some don't. We can help you have that conversation.

My family doesn't all agree. What happens if they inherit the land?

Any deed-level restriction or transfer runs with the land and binds future owners, regardless of their wishes. If family agreement is uncertain, a conservation easement or transfer needs careful timing and legal planning. An MOU would give you flexibility to work with us now without locking your heirs in.

Can CC help pay for any of this?

In some cases, yes. CC's Commons Fund can cover title search, survey, and closing costs for qualifying projects. We also work to identify grant funding for landowner costs on high-priority sites. Ask us early — we cannot help at the closing table what we didn't know about six months before.

Is Cultivated Commons accredited?

CC is a forming community land trust currently pursuing accreditation through the Land Trust Alliance. We operate according to LTA Standards and Practices and are working toward full accreditation. We are transparent about where we are in that process, and we will tell you plainly what it means for the instruments we can hold.

How do I know you'll still be around in 50 years?

We can't promise the future. What we can promise is that we structure every agreement to survive us: conservation easements and deed restrictions are enforceable by the state even if CC dissolves; property we hold in fee simple is protected by our governing documents from being sold or developed. And we are building the organizational capacity — board, endowment, community support — to be here for a long time. The land we tend together deserves nothing less.
